Draft v1

Evals from first principles

You changed your chatbot's instructions on Friday. How do you know it's better on Monday? This post answers that one question step by step. Where an idea is easier to try than to read, there's a small interactive demo.

1Is Monday's bot better?

Say we run a support chatbot that answers refund questions. On Friday we change its instructions (the prompt). On Monday someone asks: is it better now?

Most of us would ask it a few questions and see if the answers look okay. That's a fine start. It's actually a test, just a tiny one done by hand. The trouble is that we change the bot every week, there are hundreds of kinds of questions, and "looks okay" means different things to different people.

It's like tasting soup while cooking. One sip tells you something. But if you're running a restaurant, you need a better way to check every pot. An eval (short for evaluation) is that better way: a repeatable test for an AI system.

2Three ingredients

When we "check the bot's answers against the right answers", we're really using three things:

  1. Questions. What we ask the bot.
  2. Right answers. What a good reply looks like. For a support bot, these already exist in the help-center articles.
  3. What we care about. Is it correct? Is it clear? Does it sound like us?

Then we need someone or something to do the checking, a grader. Put these together and we have a test we can rerun every time we change the bot. Think of it as an exam: the questions, an answer key, a marking scheme, and a teacher who marks the papers.

3What does "check" mean?

The help article says "Refunds are available within 30 days of purchase." The bot says "You can get your money back if it's been less than a month."

Is that right? Most of us would say "30 days" is better, but "a month" isn't wrong. Notice that we didn't say just right or wrong. We gave it something like a B+. Keep that in mind.

Checking 500 answers by hand after every change isn't realistic, so we hand the job to a grader. There are two kinds:

  • An exact-word check. A small piece of code that looks for specific words, like "30 days". It's instant and free, and it gives the same result every time. But it's like a spell-checker: it only sees words, not meaning. "A month" fails.
  • An AI judge. A second AI reads the question, the right answer and the bot's answer, and gives a score using our marking scheme. It understands that "a month" and "30 days" mean roughly the same thing. But it's an AI too, so it makes mistakes. (People call this LLM-as-judge.)

This “AI judge” is a few simple rules pretending to be an AI, so it can run in your browser. It reads time periods the way a person would (“a month”, “four weeks”, “thirty days”).

The two graders make different mistakes. The exact-word check is too picky about wording. The AI judge can be too forgiving. So we use both. When something must be said exactly, like a phone number or a price, we use the exact check. When it needs judgment, we use the AI judge.

4Same answer every time ≠ right answer

So the AI judge gives an answer 4 out of 5. Should we believe it?

A tempting idea: run the judge ten times, and if it says 4 every time, it must be good. But think about a bathroom scale that always says 72 kg when you actually weigh 69. It gives the same number every time, and that number is always wrong.

So there are two different questions. Does it give the same answer every time? And is that answer right? Running the judge again and again only answers the first.

Think of darts. Your throws can land close together but far from the bullseye. Or they can be scattered all over, but centered on the bullseye on average. Play with it:

5Who checks the judge?

It's tempting to say the help article is the bag of rice. But there are two different checks here, and the article only helps with one:

What we checkAgainst whatWho provides it
The bot's answerThe right answerHelp article

The article says "30 days". It can't tell you whether "a month" deserves a 5, a 4 or a fail. A person has to decide that. So the bag of rice is a set of answers that an expert has already scored by hand. Then we run the AI judge on the same answers and see how often it agrees with the expert.

It's like hiring a new teacher. Before trusting them with the whole class, you give them a few papers you've already marked and see if their marks match yours.

Try itYou vs. the AI judge

The expert's marked answers can be used in two ways:

  • To teach the judge. Show it some marked examples so it learns what a 4 looks like.
  • To test the judge. See how often it agrees with the expert.

Never test with the same examples you taught with. That's like giving students the exam questions the night before: a perfect score only proves they memorised the answers. Keep them apart, for example 20 marked answers for teaching and 30 different ones for testing. (In machine learning this is called a train/test split.)

6When everyone is wrong together

Could the AI judge and the expert agree and both be wrong? Yes. It happens when both of them check against the same help article, and that article is out of date.

Picture a group of friends all using the same old map. They all agree on the route, and they all end up at a shop that closed last year.

Try itEveryone trusts the same article

An eval tells you whether the bot agrees with your right answers. It doesn't tell you whether those answers are still true. If the help article is out of date, the eval says everything is fine while customers get wrong answers. Keeping the right answers up to date is part of running an eval.

7Where do the test questions come from?

If we write the test questions ourselves, they come out neat: "What is your refund policy?" Real customers write "bought it 5 wks ago, broken, $$ back??". So the questions should come from real chats, taken from the bot's logs.

But if we just pick chats at random, we mostly get easy questions, because most customers ask easy things. Say we pick 500 and get 400 "where is my refund?", 90 "how long do I have?", and only 10 hard ones: charged twice, an item that arrived broken after the return window closed, a customer about to dispute the charge with their bank. The bot scores 97%. Should we celebrate?

Think of a restaurant where 97% of dishes come out great. Sounds good, until you learn the 3% that go wrong are the orders for people with food allergies.

Try itWhat the average hides

The fix is to score each group of questions separately, instead of one big average. Two more habits help. Add extra hard questions on purpose: find them in complaints and conversations that got passed to a human, and write some yourself if you can't find enough. And care most about the mistakes that cost the most, not the ones that happen most often. One wrong answer about a bank dispute can hurt more than fifty slightly vague answers about refund status.

8Did it actually get better?

We fix the prompt and test the 10 hard questions again. The score goes from 4 out of 10 to 6 out of 10. Is it better?

A good first step is to look at each question, not just the total. Which questions changed from fail to pass, and do they stay that way? If question #7 failed every time before and passes every time now, that's a real fix. If it passed only sometimes before and still passes only sometimes, nothing really changed.

Running the test several times and averaging also helps, but it only deals with one of two kinds of luck:

  1. The bot's luck. Ask the same question twice and you can get two different answers. Running each question several times evens this out.
  2. The luck of which questions you picked. We only have 10 hard questions. A different 10 might have shown no improvement at all. Rerunning the same 10 doesn't fix this. Only more questions does.

It's like flipping a coin. Get 7 heads out of 10 flips and you can't say the coin is unfair, because that happens often enough by chance. Get 140 heads out of 200 flips and now you can.

9Not enough hard questions? Make some up

So 10 hard questions is too few to trust. But hard questions are rare by nature. Last month only 10 customers were about to dispute a charge with their bank. We can't wait a year for 200 more.

The answer is to make some up, usually by asking an AI to write them. (This is called synthetic data.) But remember the problem from section 7: made-up questions come out neat and polite, and real customers don't write like that. Four habits keep fake questions useful:

  1. Start from real examples. Show the AI your 10 real hard questions, typos and all, so the fake ones sound like real people.
  2. Mix the ingredients on purpose. Don't just say "write hard questions". List the parts and combine them, so you get variety you chose instead of variety the AI happened to come up with.
  3. Have an expert check them. Some will be impossible, too easy, or have the wrong right answer.
  4. Keep the real ones separate. Use the fake questions for everyday testing, and save the real ones as the final check. If the bot passes 95% of the fake questions and fails the real ones, your fake questions aren't realistic enough.

10Learn from real chats

Say the eval says Monday's bot is better, but this week more customers asked to talk to a human. Does that mean the bot got worse?

Not necessarily. It's a clue, not a verdict. The way to find out is to read those chats. They usually fall into one of three buckets:

  • The help articles don't have the answer. The bot is fine; there's a gap in the articles.
  • The bot answered badly. The eval missed a kind of mistake.
  • The customer just wanted a human. That's nobody's fault.

Whichever bucket they fall into, those chats are exactly the questions the eval doesn't have yet. So they go into the test. An eval isn't something you build once. It grows from what goes wrong in real chats.

11Teaching to the test

Now the team has a good eval. Everyone tweaks the prompt, runs the eval, keeps the tweak if the score went up, and repeats. After 50 tweaks the score has climbed from 70% to 94%. Is the bot really 24 points better?

Probably not. Two things are going on:

  1. The prompt learns the test. Each tweak gets chosen because it helps these exact questions. It's a student who has memorised last year's exam: great on that paper, no better on a new one. (In machine learning this is called overfitting.)
  2. We keep the lucky results. In section 8 we saw that scores go up and down by luck. Try 50 tweaks and keep only the ones that scored higher, and some of those "wins" were just lucky runs.

The fix is to keep a locked set of questions that nobody tunes on. You only check it once in a while, like a final exam nobody has seen. Keep adding fresh real chats to it, and keep watching what real customers do. There's a famous saying for this, known as Goodhart's law: when a number becomes the goal, it stops being a good measure.
