Evals from first principles

You changed the prompt on Friday. How do you know Monday's bot is better? This post builds up evals from that one question, without the jargon. Wherever an idea is easier to feel than to read, there's a playground.

1Is Monday's bot better?

Say we have a support bot that answers refund questions. On Friday we tweak the prompt. On Monday someone asks: is it better now?

The honest first answer is that we try a few questions and eyeball it. That's not wrong. It's an eval, just a tiny one that we run by hand and keep in our head. The trouble starts when we need to do it again next Friday, and the Friday after, across hundreds of questions, and when "looks fine" from two different people means two different things.

So let's take the eyeball test apart and see what it's made of.

2Three pieces

When we "compare against the ideal answer", we're really using three things:

  1. Inputs. The questions we ask the bot.
  2. References. What a good answer looks like. For a support bot these already exist: the knowledge articles.
  3. Criteria. What we check: correctness, consistency, writing style.

Add something that applies the criteria, a checker, and we have a loop we can run after every change.

3What does "compare" mean?

The reference says "Refunds are available within 30 days of purchase." The bot says "You can get your money back if it's been less than a month."

Is that correct? Most of us would say: 30 days is better, but a month isn't wrong. Look at what we just did. We didn't give a pass or a fail. We gave a grade. Keep that in mind.

To do this 500 times, we have two kinds of checker:

  • Deterministic checks like regex and exact match. They're fast, free and never change their mind. They're also literal: "a month" fails a check for "30 days".
  • LLM-as-judge. We give a second model the rubric, the reference and the output, and ask for a grade. It understands paraphrase, but it's a model too, so it can be wrong.

The judge here is a small rule-based stand-in for an LLM, so it runs in your browser. It reads durations the way a person would (“a month”, “four weeks”, “thirty days”).

The two checkers fail differently. Regex is too strict about wording. The judge can be too lenient about meaning. In practice we use both. Facts that must appear word for word, like a phone number, a legal phrase or a policy figure, go to deterministic checks. Everything that needs judgment goes to the judge.

4Consistent is not correct

So the judge gives an answer a 4. Why should we believe that 4?

A tempting answer: run it ten times, and if it says 4 every time, it's reliable. But think about a bathroom scale that reads 72 kg every time you step on it, while you actually weigh 69. It's perfectly consistent and wrong every time.

Those are two different properties. Reliability means you get the same reading every time. Validity means the reading is the right one. Running the judge many times measures the first. It says nothing about the second.

5Who judges the judge?

It's tempting to say the knowledge article is the known weight. But there are two comparisons here, and the article only covers one of them:

What we checkChecked againstComes from
The bot's answerThe ideal answerKnowledge article
The judge's gradeThe right gradeA subject-matter expert

The article says "30 days". It can't say whether "a month" deserves a 5, a 4 or a fail. A person decided that. So the known weight is a set of answers labelled by an expert. We grade them ourselves, run the judge on the same answers, and measure how often we agree.

Try it. You're the expert. Grade these eight answers to “How long do I have to return something?” against the reference “30 days of purchase”, then see what the judge said.

PlaygroundYou vs. the judge

Expert labels have two uses, and they need to stay separate:

  • To teach the judge. Put graded examples into its prompt so it learns what a 4 looks like.
  • To measure the judge. Check how often it agrees with the expert.

Never measure with the examples you taught with. If the judge has already seen the answers, agreeing with them only proves it memorised them. Split the labels, for example 20 to teach and 30 to test. It's the train/test split from machine learning, which we just rediscovered from scratch.

6When everyone is wrong together

Could the judge and the expert agree and both be wrong? Yes, whenever they share the same source of truth and that source goes stale.

PlaygroundA shared source of truth

An eval measures agreement with a reference, not truth. When the reference drifts, the eval stays green while the product is wrong. Keeping references fresh is part of the eval, not a separate chore.

7Where do the questions come from?

If we write the test questions ourselves, they come out tidy: "What is your refund policy?" Real customers write "bought it 5 wks ago, broken, $$ back??". So the inputs should come from production logs.

But a random sample of logs mirrors traffic, and traffic is mostly easy questions. Say we sample 500 and get 400 "where is my refund?", 90 "how long do I have?", and 10 hard ones: charged twice, damaged item past the window, a bank dispute. The bot scores 97%. Should we celebrate?

PlaygroundWhat the average hides

The fix is to score each slice separately. Then there are two follow-ups. Oversample the hard cases on purpose: pull them from escalations and complaints, and write some when the logs are too thin. And weight slices by what failure costs, not by how often they happen. One wrong answer about a chargeback can cost more than fifty vague ones about refund status.

8Did it actually get better?

We fix the prompt and run the hard slice again. It goes from 4/10 to 6/10. Better?

A good first move is to compare pairs, not averages. Ask which examples flipped, and whether they stay flipped. If a question failed in all 10 runs before and passes in all 10 now, that's a real fix. If it went from flaky to flaky, it isn't.

Running each version many times and comparing means also helps, but only against one of two sources of noise:

  1. Run-to-run noise. The same question gets different answers on different runs. Repeated runs average this out.
  2. Which-questions noise. We only have 10 hard questions. A different 10 might have shown no improvement at all. Re-running the same 10 doesn't help with that. Only more questions does.

With 10 questions, a real 20-point improvement fails to show up in a noticeable share of evals, and the score of a single eval can land almost anywhere. With a few hundred, the two clouds pull apart. The gap has to be large compared to the spread before we believe it.
