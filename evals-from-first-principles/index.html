Draft v2

Evals from first principles

You changed your chatbot's instructions on Friday. How do you know it's better on Monday? This post answers that one question step by step, in plain words first, then gives each idea its proper name. Where an idea is easier to try than to read, there's a demo.

1Is Monday's bot better?

Say we run a support chatbot that answers refund questions. On Friday we change its instructions (the prompt). On Monday someone asks: is it better now?

Most of us would ask it a few questions and see if the answers look okay. That's a fine start. It's actually a test, just a tiny one done by hand. The trouble is that we change the bot every week, there are hundreds of kinds of questions, and "looks okay" means different things to different people.

It's like tasting soup while cooking. One sip tells you something. But if you're running a restaurant, you need a better way to check every pot.

The proper term
Eval
Short for evaluation: a repeatable test that measures how well an AI system does its job. The rest of this post is about building one you can trust.

2Three ingredients

When we "check the bot's answers against the right answers", we're really using three things:

  1. Questions. What we ask the bot.
  2. Right answers. What a good reply looks like. For a support bot, these already exist in the help-center articles.
  3. What we care about. Is it correct? Is it clear? Does it sound like us?

Then we need someone or something to do the checking: a grader. Think of it as an exam: the questions, an answer key, a marking scheme, and a teacher who marks the papers.

3What does "check" mean?

The help article says "Refunds are available within 30 days of purchase." The bot says "You can get your money back if it's been less than a month."

Is that right? Most of us would say "30 days" is better, but "a month" isn't wrong. Notice that we didn't say just right or wrong. We gave it something like a B+. Keep that in mind.

Checking 500 answers by hand after every change isn't realistic, so we hand the job to a grader. There are two kinds:

  • An exact-word check. A small piece of code that looks for specific words, like "30 days". It's instant and free, and it gives the same result every time. But it's like a spell-checker: it only sees words, not meaning. "A month" fails.
  • An AI judge. A second AI reads the question, the right answer and the bot's answer, and gives a score using our rubric. It understands that "a month" and "30 days" mean roughly the same thing. But it's an AI too, so it makes mistakes.

Try both on the same answer. The AI judge in this demo is a simulation with four flaws built in on purpose, the kind real AI judges have. See if you can spot them. We'll catch them properly in section 5.

The judge's four flaws: it treats “four weeks” as 30 days, it gets fooled by friendly answers with no facts, it misses made-up rules, and it marks down answers that ask the customer a follow-up question. The same judge is used again in section 5.

The two graders make different mistakes. The exact-word check is too picky about wording. The AI judge can be too forgiving, or forgiving in the wrong places. So we use both. When something must be said exactly, like a phone number or a price, we use the exact check. When it needs judgment, we use the AI judge.

The proper terms
Deterministic (or code-based) check
A check written in code that gives the same result for the same input every time. Exact match and regex (a pattern-matching mini-language) are the common ones.
LLM-as-judge
Using a large language model (an LLM) as the grader.

4Same answer every time ≠ right answer

So the AI judge gives an answer 4 out of 5. Should we believe it?

A tempting idea: run the judge ten times, and if it says 4 every time, it must be good. But think about a bathroom scale that always says 72 kg when you actually weigh 69. It gives the same number every time, and that number is always wrong.

So a grader can go wrong in two separate ways. Its scores can jump around from run to run. Or they can be off in the same direction every time. Running the judge again and again only reveals the first.

The proper terms
Variance
How much the scores jump around between runs. The usual way to measure it is the standard deviation: roughly, how far a typical score lands from the average. High variance means any single run can't be trusted, but averaging many runs helps.
Bias
How far off the average score is from the correct one. A judge that's always one point too harsh has a bias of −1. Averaging more runs does nothing for bias.
Reliability vs. validity
Reliable means you get the same result every time (low variance). Valid means the result is actually correct (low bias, too). The harsh bathroom scale is reliable but not valid.

Notice the harsh judge: very steady, always wrong, and nothing in its own scores gives that away. You only saw it because the expert's 4 was marked in green. So how do you check a bathroom scale? You put something on it whose weight you already know, like a 5 kg bag of rice. What's the bag of rice for an AI judge?

5Who checks the judge?

It's tempting to say the help article is the bag of rice. But there are two different checks here, and the article only helps with one:

The article says "30 days". It can't tell you whether "a month" deserves a 5, a 4 or a fail. A person has to decide that. So the bag of rice is a set of answers that an expert has already marked by hand. Then we run the AI judge on the same answers and see how often it agrees.

It's like hiring a new teacher. Before trusting them with the whole class, you give them a few papers you've already marked and see if their marks match yours.

The judge's job here is to flag bad answers before customers see them. You're the expert. Mark each of these answers to “How long do I have to return something?” as good or bad. The right answer is “30 days of purchase.”

There are four ways each answer can end up, and each one has a name:

The proper terms
Labels / ground truth
The expert's marks. They're what we treat as correct.
Confusion matrix
The 2 × 2 table above: what the judge said versus what the expert said.
True positive / true negative
The judge got it right: it flagged a bad answer, or passed a good one. ("Positive" just means "flagged". It isn't a compliment.)
False positive
A false alarm: the judge flagged an answer that was actually fine.
False negative
A miss: a bad answer slipped through.
Precision
When the judge says “bad”, how often is it right? Flagged-and-really-bad ÷ everything flagged. Low precision means lots of false alarms.
Recall
Of all the really bad answers, how many did the judge catch? Caught ÷ all bad. Low recall means bad answers reach customers.
Accuracy
How often the judge and expert agreed, overall. Easy to read, but misleading when most answers are good: a judge that never flags anything still looks accurate.
Cohen's kappa
Agreement after removing the part you'd expect from pure luck. 1 means perfect, 0 means no better than guessing, and anything above about 0.6 is usually considered decent.
F1 score
One number that combines precision and recall. It's only high when both are high.

Play with the strictness slider after you compare. A stricter judge catches more bad answers (higher recall) but raises more false alarms (lower precision). A gentler one does the opposite. You can't max out both by changing strictness. You have to choose which mistake costs you more.

One more thing: "four weeks" is honestly debatable. If you asked two experts, they might disagree. That's normal, and it's worth measuring too. If two experts only agree 80% of the time, no judge can be expected to agree with them 95% of the time.

The proper term
Inter-annotator agreement
How often two human experts agree with each other. It sets a ceiling on how well any judge can match "the" expert.

The expert's marked answers can be used in two ways: to teach the judge (show it marked examples in its prompt), and to test it. Never test with the same examples you taught with. That's like giving students the exam questions the night before: a perfect score only proves they memorised them. Keep them apart, for example 20 marked answers for teaching and 30 different ones for testing.

The proper term
Train/test split
Keeping the examples you learn or tune from separate from the examples you measure with.

6When everyone is wrong together

Could the AI judge and the expert agree and both be wrong? Yes. It happens when both of them check against the same help article, and that article is out of date.

Picture a group of friends all using the same old map. They all agree on the route, and they all end up at a shop that closed last year.

ExampleEveryone trusts the same article

An eval tells you whether the bot agrees with your right answers. It doesn't tell you whether those answers are still true. Keeping them up to date is part of running an eval.

The proper term
Stale ground truth (a kind of drift)
The world changed, but your "right answers" didn't. Drift is the general name for the real world moving away from what your test assumes.

7Where do the test questions come from?

If we write the test questions ourselves, they come out neat: "What is your refund policy?" Real customers write "bought it 5 wks ago, broken, $$ back??". So the questions should come from real chats, taken from the bot's logs.

But if we just pick chats at random, we mostly get easy questions, because most customers ask easy things. Say we pick 500 and get 400 "where is my refund?", 90 "how long do I have?", and only 10 hard ones: charged twice, an item that arrived broken after the return window closed, a customer about to dispute the charge with their bank. The bot scores 97%. Should we celebrate?

Think of a restaurant where 97% of dishes come out great. Sounds good, until you learn the 3% that go wrong are the orders for people with food allergies.

Try itWhat the average hides

The fix is to score each group of questions separately, instead of one big average. Two more habits help. Add extra hard questions on purpose, instead of leaving it to chance. And care most about the mistakes that cost the most, not the ones that happen most often. One wrong answer about a bank dispute can hurt more than fifty slightly vague answers about refund status.

The proper terms
Distribution
The mix of questions: how often each kind shows up. Random sampling copies the real mix, easy questions and all.
Slice
One group of questions scored on its own, like "hard cases".
Stratified sampling
Picking test questions group by group, so small groups get enough questions to measure.
Cost-weighted scoring
Counting expensive mistakes more heavily than cheap ones.

8Did it actually get better?

We fix the prompt and test the 10 hard questions again. The score goes from 4 out of 10 to 6 out of 10. Is it better?

A good first step is to look at each question, not just the total. Which questions changed from fail to pass, and do they stay that way? If question #7 failed every time before and passes every time now, that's a real fix. If it passed only sometimes before and still passes only sometimes, nothing really changed.

Running the test several times and averaging also helps, but it only deals with one of two kinds of luck:

  1. The bot's luck. Ask the same question twice and you can get two different answers. Running each question several times evens this out.
  2. The luck of which questions you picked. We only have 10 hard questions. A different 10 might have shown no improvement at all. Rerunning the same 10 doesn't fix this. Only more questions does.

It's like flipping a coin. Get 7 heads out of 10 flips and you can't say the coin is unfair, because that happens often enough by chance. Get 140 heads out of 200 flips and now you can.

The proper terms
Sample size
How many questions are in the eval. Usually written n.
Sampling error
The up-and-down in the score that comes purely from which questions happened to be picked.
Confidence interval
A range that the true score probably sits in. The demo's "middle 95%" works like a 95% confidence interval. It gets narrower as n grows, roughly halving each time you have four times as many questions.
Statistically significant
The gap is big enough that luck alone is an unlikely explanation.
Paired comparison
Comparing old and new on the same questions, one question at a time. Because it removes the "which questions" luck, it spots real changes with fewer questions.

9Not enough hard questions? Make some up

So 10 hard questions is too few to trust. But hard questions are rare by nature. Last month only 10 customers were about to dispute a charge with their bank. We can't wait a year for 200 more.

The answer is to make some up, usually by asking an AI to write them. But remember the problem from section 7: made-up questions come out neat and polite, and real customers don't write like that. Four habits keep fake questions useful:

  1. Start from real examples. Show the AI your 10 real hard questions, typos and all, so the fake ones sound like real people.
  2. Mix the ingredients on purpose. List the parts and combine them, so you get variety you chose instead of variety the AI happened to come up with.
  3. Have an expert check them. Some will be impossible, too easy, or have the wrong right answer.
  4. Keep the real ones separate. Use the fake questions for everyday testing and save the real ones as the final check. If the bot passes 95% of the fake questions and fails the real ones, your fake questions aren't realistic enough.

The proper terms
Synthetic data
Test questions (or answers) that were generated instead of collected from real use.
Coverage
How many of the situations you care about the eval actually tests.

10Learn from real chats

Say the eval says Monday's bot is better, but this week more customers asked to talk to a human. Does that mean the bot got worse?

Not necessarily. It's a clue, not a verdict. The way to find out is to read those chats. They usually fall into one of three buckets:

  • The help articles don't have the answer. The bot is fine; there's a gap in the articles.
  • The bot answered badly. The eval missed a kind of mistake.
  • The customer just wanted a human. That's nobody's fault.

Whichever bucket they fall into, those chats are exactly the questions the eval doesn't have yet. So they go into the test. An eval isn't something you build once. It grows from what goes wrong in real chats.

Online eval (or monitoring)

11Teaching to the test

Now the team has a good eval. Everyone tweaks the prompt, runs the eval, keeps the tweak if the score went up, and repeats. After 50 tweaks the score has climbed from 70% to 94%. Is the bot really 24 points better?

Probably not. Two things are going on:

  1. The prompt learns the test. Every tweak helps some questions and hurts others. We keep the tweaks that happen to suit these exact questions. It's a student who has memorised last year's exam: great on that paper, no better on a new one.
  2. We keep the lucky results. In section 8 we saw that scores go up and down by luck. Try 50 tweaks and keep only the ones that scored higher, and some of those "wins" were just lucky runs.

The proper terms
Overfitting
Getting better at the specific test questions without getting better at the job.
Held-out set
The locked set of questions that nobody tunes on, kept back to measure honestly.
Goodhart's law
When a number becomes the goal, it stops being a good measure.
